Referral Rewards System

Context

This product case study for Redo explores how a referral rewards feature could work, why it matters to merchants, and how it could be positioned as a real product.

Role

Project manager

Duration

January 2026 to May 2026

Team

Sejal Bilwar, Eric Lan, Alexa Kaur Sidhu, Leinita Panda, Lindsay Lai, Selina Zhang

Tools

Research, prototyping, Cursor

Project Overview

We treated Referral Reward like a real product, not just a feature idea.

This project started with a broad prompt from Redo: explore whether a referral reward feature could become a meaningful extension of their post-purchase platform. Instead of jumping straight into UI, we structured the work like a full case study, beginning with merchant pain points, framing a product strategy, and then translating those insights into a prototype and launch story.

As co-project manager, I helped shape the initial scope, organize the team’s deliverables, and make sure the research, product thinking, and presentation all connected into one cohesive narrative.

Problem Space

The post-purchase moment is valuable, but most merchants still do not capture referral intent well.

The original PRD positioned Referral Reward around a simple idea: right after a customer completes a purchase, they are at a high point of satisfaction and more likely to share a brand they trust. The challenge is that existing referral tools often feel bolted on, overly complex, or disconnected from the actual checkout experience.

Redo’s opportunity was to turn that brief post-purchase window into a structured acquisition channel with a setup flow simple enough for busy Shopify merchants to actually use.

Project Context

Redo already owned the post-purchase experience, which made referrals a natural next step.

Redo is an ecommerce software company focused on what happens after checkout: returns, exchanges, support, and order tracking. Their broader mission is to reduce friction, save merchants time, and strengthen customer loyalty.

That context mattered. Rather than imagining a referral tool in isolation, we were designing a feature that could live inside an ecosystem merchants already use to manage retention and customer relationships.

Scope

We defined the project around three connected deliverables.

In our initial project plan, my co-project manager and I broke the work into three tracks that would reinforce one another instead of drifting apart.

  • Merchant research to validate whether the concept solved a real business problem
  • A marketing website to position Referral Reward like a launch-ready Redo product
  • A working prototype to show how the merchant and customer flows would function

This structure helped us stay grounded in strategy while still building tangible artifacts.

Our Process

We built the case study in stages so each phase could sharpen the next one.

The final deck summarized the project as a timeline, but in practice this process helped us make better decisions at each step. We used qualitative discovery to understand merchants, synthesized those learnings into product and marketing choices, validated the core assumptions through prototype thinking, and then packaged everything into a clear final story.

Research Setup

We began by defining what we actually needed to learn from merchants.

The research packet and kickoff notes made one thing clear: merchants would not give us the product solution for free. Our job was to understand the pain points behind acquisition, loyalty, and post-purchase engagement, then use those patterns to shape the feature.

Our research plan focused on questions like:

  • How do merchants currently acquire new customers?
  • What does their thank-you page look like today, and what tools already live there?
  • Have they tried referral programs before, and what made those succeed or fail?
  • What kinds of rewards feel motivating without cutting too deeply into margins?
  • What would make a new Shopify plug-in feel worth installing?

Research Goals

We grounded the work in merchant behavior, not assumptions about what referrals should look like.

Merchant interviews

We planned targeted conversations with Shopify merchants to hear how they talked about acquisition, referrals, loyalty, and post-purchase tools in their own words.

Competitor analysis

We studied returns, loyalty, and referral products to understand what was already in market, where the flow broke down, and how Redo could feel differentiated.

Persona and product framing

Our initial plan also called for a product decisions document that would connect merchant needs to a clear target segment, pain points, and feature choices.

Synthesis

Our early synthesis showed that successful referral systems need more than a reward.

Looking across research articles, market examples, and our team synthesis, a few consistent themes emerged. Referral programs work best when they feel easy to use, clearly promoted, and worth sharing. They also become stronger when the reward feels somewhat exclusive rather than generic.

We also noted that gamification only becomes meaningful when users can see progress. A reward alone is not enough; the system needs visibility, momentum, and a reason to come back.

Merchant Insights

Merchant feedback gave us a more realistic view of where referrals help and where they fall flat.

The interviews pushed us away from a one-size-fits-all assumption. Some brands felt referrals would not work because their products were too niche or already driven by organic trust. Others saw strong potential, especially when discoverability had become a challenge.

  • Merchants wanted clearer tracking and analytics around referrals
  • They were more open to the feature when there was evidence it could actually drive reach
  • Many teams still rely heavily on paid channels like Meta and Google for scaling

What We Learned

Three strategic tensions shaped the rest of the product direction.

Referrals are not equally valuable for every merchant

Community-driven and highly referable brands may benefit more than niche or strongly organic brands that already convert through trust alone.

Ease of setup is part of the value proposition

Merchants choose Redo because it reduces operational burden. If referrals required too much customization or technical help, the concept would lose its edge.

Proof and transparency matter

Merchants were interested in referrals when performance could be tracked clearly, rewards felt financially sensible, and the flow did not create abuse or confusion.

Frameworks

We turned the raw research into a strategic lens using SWOT.

The SWOT analysis helped us move from scattered observations into product strategy. We mapped what already made Redo attractive, where the product still felt weak, where the market was open, and what risks could keep a referral product from gaining traction.

This reframed the project from “build a referral tool” into “design a referral system that fits Redo’s business model, merchant expectations, and product positioning.”

Opportunity Areas

From that strategy work, we narrowed the concept into four concrete opportunity areas.

Formalize word of mouth

Merchants already believed referrals create purchases, but many lacked the structure to capture and reward that behavior systematically.

Prioritize easy setup and transparency

Redo needed to feel lightweight and merchant-friendly, especially for brands that do not want to hire a developer or manage another complex tool.

Use re-engagement automation

Redo’s existing email and SMS capabilities suggested a path to support referrals and win-back moments without forcing a totally separate ecosystem.

Focus on community-oriented brands

Some merchants are especially well-positioned for referrals because customer identity, loyalty, and community already overlap.

Product Definition

The PRD translated research into a merchant-facing flow that felt configurable but simple.

The original product definition placed Referral Reward under Marketing > Loyalty inside the Redo merchant portal. The core concept was bilateral rewards: when a customer refers a friend who purchases, both sides receive a benefit.

Key decisions from the PRD included:

  • Reward types: percentage refund, fixed amount refund, or free product
  • Merchant controls for referral window, eligible products, and reward differences
  • A thank-you page widget that appears immediately after checkout
  • Copy-link and copy-message actions to make sharing low-friction

Merchant setup

Configure rewards, timing, and eligibility from one dashboard.

Customer moment

Present the referral offer exactly when purchase satisfaction is highest.

Reward logic

Trigger rewards only after the referred friend completes a successful purchase.

Marketing Website

We built the website to sell the concept clearly before showing technical depth.

The deck framed the website as more than a portfolio artifact. It needed to position Referral Reward like something Redo could actually launch: credible, easy to understand, and directly tied to merchant value.

That meant leading with clarity over complexity. The site needed to explain the concept fast, establish trust, and show enough of the flow that merchants could imagine adopting it.

Website Structure

The landing page was organized to move from value proposition to proof.

The structure emphasized a clear hero section, a product demo, the referral flow, tiered rewards, benefits, responsive design, and repeated calls to action. Visually, the site leaned on a clean layout and strong conversion moments so the idea felt polished and launch-ready rather than speculative.

Feature Prototype

In parallel, we built the concept into a working system architecture.

The prototype work focused on proving the core mechanics: merchant onboarding, customer sharing behavior, referral capture, and automated reward delivery. This was important because the website could sell the concept, but the prototype had to show that the flow actually made sense as a product.

The final deck grouped the prototype into three parts: the merchant dashboard, the customer experience, and the referral engine connecting them.

Core Features

The system centered on four features that made the referral loop usable for both merchants and shoppers.

Referral link generation

Generate a unique, shareable referral code for each customer after purchase.

Merchant controls

Let merchants configure reward type, amount, time window, and product eligibility.

Automated reward triggers

Issue rewards once a referred order is completed, rather than relying on manual tracking.

Real-time tracking

Surface referral status, conversion rate, and issued rewards in a live dashboard.

Prototype Flow

We mapped the end-to-end referral flow from merchant setup to reward issuance.

This flow made the product logic tangible. It clarified what merchants needed to configure, when the customer would see the referral offer, how the referred friend’s purchase would be captured, and where the system would automate the reward.

Outcome

The final deliverable connected research, positioning, and product logic into one coherent story.

By the end of the project, we had not just proposed a feature. We had shown where the idea fit within Redo’s existing platform, why certain merchants would care, what the experience should look like, and how the concept could be communicated as a viable product.

That process mattered as much as the final output. The strongest part of this project was the way research informed strategy, strategy shaped the prototype, and the prototype then strengthened the final narrative.
